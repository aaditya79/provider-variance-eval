\begin{abstract}
Researchers increasingly evaluate open-weight language models through
API aggregators that route a single model name to many third-party
providers, each serving the same weights under different
infrastructure, quantization, and configuration. We ask whether this
invisible routing threatens the validity of API-based evaluation, and
introduce a pre-registered harness that separates two properties prior
work has conflated: whether a provider returns a correct answer, and
whether it returns a complete one. Across two open models (12 and 6
live providers respectively) and three benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU,
HumanEval), what can be claimed about accuracy turns out to depend on
which test the protocol specifies. The same observations yield an
affirmative equivalence result on one benchmark (invariance within
$\pm$3 percentage points), no evidence in either direction on a
second, and on the third an uncorrected paired signal exceeding the
pre-registered threshold. We report the three separately rather than
as one verdict, and treat that dependence as a finding in itself.
Reliability is a cleaner story. One provider (anonymized as
Provider~C for review) shows a serving-level truncation pathology
absent from every other provider we test, on both 70B benchmarks
(16.2\% and 7.9\% against a peer maximum of 0.0\%, reproduced at 20\%
on a disjoint re-run), reported overtly as
\texttt{finish\_reason=error}. It is not explained by output length,
and is deterministic and item-specific: at temperature 0 a fixed
subset of GSM8K items accounts for most truncations, with 31 of 300
items failing on all three repeated trials against approximately 1.3
expected by chance. We quantify this by applying ICC(1,1) to the
truncation indicator (0.698 and 0.751 on the two affected cells). We
further show that two apparent reliability findings dissolve under
audit: every silent-truncation candidate our detector flagged on a
second model was a complete, correct answer misclassified by a
formatting heuristic, and Provider~C's apparent 13.8\% silent-failure
rate on MMLU/8B is an artifact of how \texttt{finish\_reason} renders
a failure that peers also exhibit. A survey of eight candidate models
finds only two with enough multi-provider coverage for this kind of
study, itself evidence that wide multi-provider serving concentrates
on a few popular models. We release our harness, a reporting
checklist, and all raw per-call logs with checksums (link in the
Ethics Statement).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A benchmark score reported for an open-weight model such as
Llama-3.3-70B is often treated as a property of the model. In
practice, when that model is accessed through an API aggregator such
as OpenRouter, the score is a property of the model, the serving
provider, the disclosed or undisclosed quantization, and the date the
call was made. Aggregators route a single model identifier across many
third-party providers, and those providers differ in hardware,
precision, batching policy, and software stack. A recent measurement
study of hosted open-weight APIs documents exactly this divergence
across providers serving a common model identifier
\citep{same_model_service2026}, and work on reproducible evaluation
has independently found that implementation-level details of an
evaluation harness materially change reported benchmark numbers
\citep{biderman2024lessons}.

Two questions follow from this. First, does provider routing change
what a benchmark measures: does accuracy actually vary across
providers serving the same weights, enough to change which model looks
better. Second, and less studied, does provider routing change whether
a benchmark measurement is trustworthy at all: can a provider fail to
complete a generation in a way that a standard accuracy harness
silently records as a wrong answer rather than a missing one. We treat
these as two distinct axes, accuracy and reliability, and build a
pre-registered measurement protocol that reports both.

On the first axis our answer is deliberately not a single verdict. What
can be claimed about accuracy depends on which statistical test the
protocol specifies, and our data make that dependence concrete rather
than hypothetical: the same observations support an affirmative
equivalence claim on one benchmark, a bare failure to reject on
another, and a flagged, uncorrected signal of separation on a third
(Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}). We treat this as a methodological
finding in its own right, since a study that reported only the
pre-registered unpaired test would have called all three ``invariant.''
Our own initial data also made a compelling case for provider-driven
accuracy variance until we discovered that the apparent effect was a
truncation artifact, not a real difference. The second axis is
cleaner. One provider in our set exhibits an overt truncation
pathology that no other provider shows, on both benchmarks where its
70B deployment generates long outputs, re-run on a disjoint item
sample, and traceable to specific input items rather than to random
infrastructure noise.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt, parsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{A two-axis measurement protocol.} We separate answer
  correctness from generation completeness, with a pre-registered
  taxonomy of failure types (overt error, silently mislabeled, and
  our own token-budget artifact) and an audit step that verifies every
  automatically flagged failure against the raw completion text before
  it is counted.

\item \textbf{An audited empirical result.} One provider shows a real,
  re-run, content-triggered reliability failure that we rule out as a
  length effect and localize to a reproducible subset of inputs, which
  we quantify by applying ICC(1,1) \citep{shrout1979icc} to the
  truncation indicator and reporting it as a Failure Consistency
  Coefficient (0.698 and 0.751 on the two affected cells).

\item \textbf{Test choice as a first-class reporting decision.} We show
  on our own data that paired versus unpaired analysis, and
  CI-overlap versus equivalence testing, change the conclusion a
  reader would draw about provider-invariance on two of three
  benchmarks. Reporting an accuracy null without stating the
  equivalence margin and the minimum detectable effect is therefore
  not sufficient \citep{card2020power, lakens2017tost}.

\item \textbf{A demonstrated need for the audit step.} We report three
  cases where an automated or self-reported failure signal produced
  findings that did not survive audit: two benchmarks where our
  detector flagged complete, correct answers using a non-standard
  closing phrase, and one cell where an apparent 13.8\% provider
  failure rate proved to be a \texttt{finish\_reason} rendering
  artifact of a failure mode peers share.

\item \textbf{A public harness and reporting checklist.} We release
  the measurement harness, frozen benchmark item sets, provider
  snapshots, and checksummed raw logs for all reported cells, together
  with a short checklist for reporting API-based evaluation results.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Cross-provider variation.} Two lines of work measure how the
same model differs across providers, and neither measures what we do.
Concurrent work treats each hosted deployment as a distinct service
object, comparing price, latency, context length, and protocol
behavior \citep{same_model_service2026}; it explicitly treats those
comparisons as weaker than a controlled experiment and does not test
accuracy equivalence. Work in a finance setting measures cross-provider
output consistency with identity and drift metrics, finding some
models stable across deployments and others severely
drift-prone \citep{output_drift2025}; it is domain-scoped and asks
whether outputs match each other, not whether a generation completed
or whether the answer is correct. We measure the dimension both leave
open: whether provider choice changes what a benchmark reports, and
whether a provider can fail in a way a standard harness silently
misgrades.

\textbf{Quantization and accuracy.} \citet{quantization_accuracy2025}
establish, under controlled conditions across a large sweep of
quantization formats, that reduced-precision serving trades accuracy
against throughput in ways that depend on the format and the task. Our contribution is not
this mechanism, which we do not dispute, but an in-the-wild
demonstration that once quantization and configuration are hidden
behind a single model name on a real aggregator, the accuracy effect
we actually observe is negligible, while an unrelated reliability
effect is not. We explicitly test and reject an output-length
explanation for our reliability finding, which rules out the simplest
version of a quantization-driven capacity story.

\textbf{Determinism at temperature zero.} Recent systems work, reported
as a technical blog post rather than an archival paper, shows that
temperature-zero inference is not bit-identical across repeated calls
because of batch-size-dependent reduction kernels, and provides
batch-invariant kernels that restore exact reproducibility at a
throughput cost \citep{thinking_machines2025}. That line of work is
about output-level nondeterminism: the same prompt can produce
different tokens on different calls. Our finding is the reverse
pattern at the level of failures: a specific provider fails on the
same items every time at temperature zero, which is evidence of a
deterministic, content-triggered defect rather than the kind of
random batch-composition noise that motivates batch-invariant
kernels.

\textbf{Benchmark auditing, fragility, and reporting.} Work on
contamination detection shows that popular auditing methods themselves
fail under distribution shift and at benchmark scale
\citep{reliability_gap_audit2026}; we take our audit step in the same
spirit, treating an automated flag as a candidate rather than a
finding, and showing concretely what happens when that principle is
skipped. Benchmark conclusions are fragile in a related way:
\citet{alzahrani2024benchmarks} find leaderboard rankings moving by as
many as eight positions under perturbations as minor as reordering
multiple-choice options, and HELM \citep{liang2023helm} argues for
standardized reporting of evaluation conditions. Provider identity is
a condition of exactly that kind which current practice does not
capture, and Section~\ref{sec:checklist} adds it to that tradition
rather than replacing it.

\textbf{Statistical practice in NLP evaluation.} \citet{card2020power}
show that NLP comparisons are routinely underpowered and that a
failure to reject is frequently reported as though it were evidence of
no difference, and \citet{dror2018hitchhiker} document how often
significance testing is omitted or misapplied in the field. Neither
question is settled by a larger benchmark: establishing the
\emph{absence} of a meaningful difference requires an equivalence
procedure with a stated margin \citep{lakens2017tost}, not a
non-significant result. Our accuracy analysis is written to that
standard, and Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} reports what happens when it
is applied to a result that a conventional test calls null.

\section{Method}

Every call is pinned to a specific provider, verified on return, and
routed into exactly one of four outcome classes --- clean completion,
overt failure, silent-failure candidate, or budget cap --- before any
accuracy number is computed. Clean completions feed the accuracy axis;
overt and audit-confirmed silent failures feed the reliability axis;
budget-cap cells feed neither. Silent-failure candidates are never
counted directly, but pass through a manual audit first, and those
that turn out to be complete answers are returned to the accuracy
axis. The full pipeline is illustrated in Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline}.
\subsection{Provider Pinning and Verification}

We access models through OpenRouter, an aggregator that routes a model
identifier to one of several third-party providers per request. Every
call pins a specific provider and disables fallback routing, so a
request cannot be silently rerouted, and every call in every reported
result verifies that the returned provider identifier matches the
pinned request; mismatches are logged and excluded rather than
silently retained. Before collecting data we confirmed that two
distinct pins on the same model return two distinct served-provider
identifiers, which caught a slug-naming mismatch that would otherwise
have invalidated the pinning. Each run persists every completed call
with a row-count integrity check and a manifest, so a partial or empty
log raises an error rather than being summarised as a successful run.

\subsection{Truncation Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We distinguish three reasons a generation may end without a complete
answer.

\textbf{Overt failure.} The API reports
\texttt{finish\_reason=error}. The provider is explicitly signaling
that it did not complete the generation.

\textbf{Silent failure.} The API reports
\texttt{finish\_reason=stop}, but the completion lacks the expected
answer marker and ends mid-token or mid-sentence. The provider is
implicitly claiming a complete generation that is not complete.

\textbf{Budget cap.} The generation reaches our configured
\texttt{max\_tokens} limit before producing an answer marker. This is
an artifact of our own configuration, not a provider property, and we
exclude it uniformly rather than attributing it to any provider. We
stress \emph{uniformly}: the exclusion is applied on every benchmark
and to every provider, including cases where one provider's cap-hits
are labelled differently by the API than another's. An earlier version
of this analysis applied the rule to GSM8K but graded MMLU cap-hits as
wrong answers, which inflated the apparent MMLU spread from 2.35 to
4.5 percentage points; Section~\ref{sec:finish_reason} shows that the
same inconsistency, applied to the reliability axis, manufactures an
entire provider-specific failure rate that does not exist.

Both overt and silent failures are excluded from the accuracy
numerator and denominator, retried under a bounded backoff policy, and
reported separately as a reliability rate.

\subsection{Audit Protocol}

Automated silent-failure detection uses a benchmark-specific
completeness heuristic: for GSM8K, whether the completion contains the
expected answer delimiter; for MMLU, whether a single letter answer is
present. This heuristic is not perfectly reliable. Before any
silent-failure count is reported in this paper, every flagged
completion was manually read against the raw text and independently
checked with a second automated classifier that inspects the full
completion rather than only the delimiter. Section~\ref{sec:audit}
reports what this audit found.

\subsection{Benchmarks}

We evaluate three benchmarks at temperature 0 with 3 repeats per
provider, using standard setups: \textbf{GSM8K}
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, 8-shot chain-of-thought over 300 items with
the \texttt{\#\#\#\#} delimiter; \textbf{MMLU}
\citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, 5-shot letter-only answers over 300 items
stratified across all 57 subjects; and \textbf{HumanEval}
\citep{chen2021humaneval}, all 164 items by raw function completion,
graded by the standard harness in a sandboxed subprocess. One
non-standard choice matters downstream: we first ran MMLU with an
8-token budget, which produced many unparseable outputs rather than
low accuracy, and raised it to 64 tokens before reporting any MMLU
result, at which point unparseable rates fell to near zero across all
providers. That 64-token cap is what Section~\ref{sec:mmlu8b} turns
on.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Model Selection}

We surveyed eight candidate open-weight models for multi-provider
coverage on OpenRouter before selecting models for full evaluation.
Only two cleared a usable threshold: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with 12
live canonical providers and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with 6. The
remaining six ranged from zero to three live endpoints, often
dominated by a single provider or by provider variants rather than
genuinely independent deployments. The full survey is in
Appendix~\ref{app:models}. We
report this survey as a finding in its own right: wide multi-provider
serving on this aggregator is concentrated on a small number of
popular models, which constrains what kind of provider-variance study
is currently possible and explains our choice of a two-model design
rather than a larger one.
\subsection{Models and Providers}

We evaluate Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct across its 12 live canonical
providers and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct across its 6 live canonical
providers. We distinguish canonical provider endpoints from
vendor-specific optimized variants (for example, speed-optimized or
region-specific routes); our primary analysis uses canonical
endpoints only, since variant routes are not independently operated
deployments in the same sense. Providers disclose a range of
quantizations at snapshot time, from fp8 through fp16, with some
providers not disclosing precision at all.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:stats}

For provider $j$ on benchmark $b$, clean-completion accuracy is
$\hat{p}_j = c_j / n_j$, where $n_j = n_j^{\text{total}} -
n_j^{\text{overt}} - n_j^{\text{silent}} - n_j^{\text{cap}}$, $c_j$ is
the number of correct answers among the $n_j$ clean completions, and
$n_j^{\text{total}}$ is the number of calls attempted. Overt failures,
audit-confirmed silent failures, and budget-cap cells are removed from
both the numerator and denominator, consistent with the taxonomy in
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}. We report 95\% Wilson score intervals for
each $\hat{p}_j$,

\[
\hat{p}_j \pm z \sqrt{\tfrac{\hat{p}_j(1-\hat{p}_j)}{n_j} + \tfrac{z^2}{4n_j^2}}
\Big/ \left(1 + \tfrac{z^2}{n_j}\right), \quad z = 1.96,
\]

\noindent rather than a normal approximation, since several providers
have $n_j$ reduced by exclusions and the Wilson interval is better
behaved near the boundary in that regime.

We pre-registered our accuracy comparison threshold before collecting
full-scale data: a provider pair is considered to show a meaningful
accuracy gap if the spread $\max_j \hat{p}_j - \min_j \hat{p}_j$ is at
least 3 percentage points \emph{and} the 95\% Wilson intervals for the
two extreme providers do not overlap. Both conditions must hold; a
large point-estimate spread with overlapping intervals is not treated
as a finding.

This criterion has two properties that Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}
shows to be consequential. Requiring two 95\% intervals to be disjoint
is a conservative proxy for a test on the difference
\citep{dror2018hitchhiker}, and treating providers as independent
discards the pairing: item sets are frozen once and reused for every
(model, provider, repeat). Relatedly, $n_j$ counts \emph{calls}, and
the three temperature-0 repeats of an item are strongly correlated, so
the effective sample size is nearer the item count.

We therefore also report a \textbf{minimum detectable effect} (MDE) at
80\% power and $\alpha = 0.05$, so that a null carries a statement of
what it could have detected \citep{card2020power}, and a \textbf{two
one-sided tests} (TOST) equivalence result \citep{lakens2017tost}
against the same 3 pp margin. Paired differences come from an
item-level bootstrap (10,000 resamples) that resamples items rather
than calls. We flag pre-registered versus post-hoc analyses
throughout, and note where a post-hoc test is uncorrected for
multiplicity.

\section{Results}

\subsection{What Can Be Claimed About Accuracy Depends on the Test}
\label{sec:accuracy}

Under the pre-registered criterion the answer is uniform and
uninteresting: on none of the three benchmarks do the two extreme
providers have disjoint 95\% Wilson intervals, so the criterion is not
met anywhere. That single sentence is what a conventional write-up
would report. It is also, we argue, insufficient, and the rest of this
section shows why: the same observations analysed with the pairing the
design already provides, and with an equivalence test rather than an
overlap heuristic, yield a materially different and \emph{benchmark-dependent}
conclusion.

On GSM8K, the benchmark with the largest number of clean completions
per provider, all nine canonical providers fall within a 1.60
percentage point band --- 97.22\% for the highest (Nebius) and 95.62\%
for the lowest (Groq) --- and every pairwise Wilson interval overlaps.
The full per-provider breakdown with intervals is in
Appendix~\ref{app:gsm8k}.
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} reports, for each benchmark, the observed
spread, the paired bootstrap interval on the extreme-pair difference,
the equivalence verdict at the pre-registered 3 pp margin, and the
minimum detectable effect. The three benchmarks do not behave alike.

\textbf{GSM8K: invariance is affirmatively supported.} The spread is
1.60 pp and the TOST procedure rejects the presence of a difference
larger than the pre-registered 3 pp margin. This is a stronger and
more informative claim than ``we failed to reject'': within
$\pm$2.94 pp, provider choice demonstrably does not move GSM8K
accuracy for this model. We regard this, not the bare null, as the
paper's accuracy result.

\textbf{MMLU: no evidence of separation, and no equivalence either.}
The spread is 2.35 pp after the Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} exclusions
are applied consistently. No pair separates under the pre-registered
criterion, but the data also do not support equivalence at 3 pp: the
margin the design can actually defend is $\pm$4.29 pp. The honest
statement is that we find no evidence of separation on MMLU, not that
MMLU is provider-invariant.

\textbf{HumanEval: a flagged, uncorrected signal.} Here the two
analyses disagree. The pre-registered unpaired criterion finds no
separation. A paired re-analysis of the extreme provider pair gives a
gap of 6.02 pp with a 95\% interval of [1.53, 10.94], which excludes
zero and exceeds the pre-registered 3 pp magnitude threshold. We do
\emph{not} report this as a confirmed accuracy difference. The extreme
pair was selected because it is extreme, and the interval is not
corrected for the 36 implicit pairwise comparisons among nine
providers; a post-hoc test on a selected maximum is anti-conservative
in exactly the way the pre-registered test is conservative. What we
claim is narrower and, we think, more useful: HumanEval accuracy is
\emph{not} shown to be provider-invariant, the design can only rule
out differences larger than $\pm$10.11 pp, and this cell warrants a
dedicated confirmatory pairwise test with a pre-specified pair, which
our protocol did not include.

\textbf{The methodological point.} These three verdicts come from one
dataset, and differ only in which test the protocol specified. Our own
pre-registered criterion would have reported all three as
``provider-invariant,'' erring in opposite directions --- overclaiming
on HumanEval, underclaiming on GSM8K, where an affirmative equivalence
result was available and went unreported. An accuracy null in an
API-based evaluation should therefore carry its equivalence margin and
minimum detectable effect, without which a reader cannot distinguish
``no difference'' from ``no power''
\citep{card2020power, lakens2017tost}.

An earlier version of our GSM8K analysis appeared to show a much
larger and statistically separated gap, driven almost entirely by one
provider. On inspection, every one of that provider's apparent errors
was a silent truncation, an incomplete generation missing the answer
delimiter, misclassified by our own harness as a wrong answer before
we corrected the taxonomy. Once truncated cells are excluded rather
than graded as incorrect, that provider's accuracy is in line with
every other provider. We report this explicitly because it is the
clearest illustration in our data of why accuracy and reliability must
be measured and audited separately: a serving-level failure, if
graded as a wrong answer, can manufacture an accuracy finding that is
not actually about accuracy at all.

\subsection{Reliability Is Not Provider-Invariant}
\label{sec:reliability}

Table~\ref{tab:anchor} is the central result of this paper. One
provider, which we refer to as Provider~C, shows an overt truncation
rate far above every other provider we test, on two of the four
benchmark-model cells where it is evaluated, and the GSM8K measurement
is reproduced on a disjoint item sample (16.2\% on the primary
900-call run, 20\% on a separately collected 100-item re-run using the
same harness). Every other provider we test, across both models and
all three benchmarks, shows an overt truncation rate of zero.

We report this table with one cell deliberately removed. An earlier
version included MMLU on the 8B model, where Provider~C appeared to
show a 13.8\% silent-failure rate against a peer maximum of 0.7\%.
Section~\ref{sec:finish_reason} shows that this apparent finding is an
artifact of \texttt{finish\_reason} labelling rather than a difference
in provider behaviour, and it has been withdrawn from both the
reliability table and the item-determinism analysis in
Appendix~\ref{app:icc}.

Provider~C's failures on the 70B model are overt: the API itself
reports \texttt{finish\_reason=error}, no answer is returned, and the
call is unambiguously identifiable as failed by any harness that
inspects the finish reason. This is the form of the pathology we
report as a finding. The 8B model is where the picture initially
looked more complicated and, on audit, turned out not to be; we treat
that separately in Section~\ref{sec:finish_reason}.

We considered and reject an explanation based on output length. Across
the cells in Table~\ref{tab:anchor}, truncation rate does not track
average completion length, and the decisive evidence is a
counterexample rather than a correlation: HumanEval on the 70B model
produces longer completions on average (415 tokens) than GSM8K (154
tokens) but shows a \emph{lower} truncation rate (7.9\% versus
16.2\%). MMLU on the 70B model produces the shortest completions of
any cell (2 tokens) and is also among the cleanest. A monotone
length-dependence account is therefore falsified outright by the
observed ordering. We previously reported a cross-cell rank
correlation in support of this conclusion; with only a handful of
cells such a coefficient has essentially no power and we do not regard
it as interpretable, so we rest the claim on the counterexample
alone. We note separately in Section~\ref{sec:determinism} that a
weak \emph{within}-cell, item-level association with output length
does exist; it does not revive the cross-cell account.

\subsection{The Pathology Is Deterministic and Item-Specific}
\label{sec:determinism}

If Provider~C's truncations were transient infrastructure noise ---
load spikes, rate-limit contention --- they would fall roughly at
random across the 300 GSM8K items and the 3 repeats of each. Under
that independent-failure null, each of an item's 3 calls truncates at
the observed base rate $p = 0.162$, so the expected number of items
with exactly $k$ truncated repeats is $E[k] = N \binom{3}{k} p^k
(1-p)^{3-k}$ with $N = 300$. Table~\ref{tab:item} compares that
expectation against what we observe.

Thirty-one items truncate on all three repeated trials, against

At temperature 0, the same input reliably produces the same failure on
this provider. This is evidence of a content-triggered, reproducible
defect rather than stochastic serving noise, and it is, to our
knowledge, the most specific characterization available of what kind
of failure this is, even without access to the underlying serving
infrastructure to identify its root cause.

The comparison above is specific to GSM8K and to a single repeat
count. To express item-level determinism in a form that generalizes
across benchmarks, we apply the one-way random-effects intraclass
correlation coefficient ICC(1,1) \citep{shrout1979icc, mcgraw1996icc}
to the truncation indicator, which we call a \textbf{Failure
Consistency Coefficient (FCC)} --- a name for an application, not a
new statistic. Provider~C's FCC is 0.698 [0.604, 0.783] on GSM8K/70B
and 0.751 [0.550, 0.887] on HumanEval/70B, the two cells where it
truncates overtly; both intervals exclude 0, confirming with a
standard reliability statistic what the binomial comparison showed for
GSM8K alone. Because the outcome is binary and $k{=}3$ is a thin basis
for an ICC, we use the coefficient only for that qualitative claim ---
failures are substantially an item property --- and not to rank cells
against one another. Appendix~\ref{app:icc} gives the definition, the
estimation procedure, the full caveats, and the withdrawal of an
earlier MMLU/8B entry.

\subsection{\texttt{finish\_reason} Is Not a Reliable Completeness
Signal}
\label{sec:finish_reason}

Two pieces of evidence in our data show that neither a provider's own
self-reported completion status, nor a naive automated completeness
check, can be trusted without verifying the actual generated text. The
first is the stronger of the two, because the finding it dissolves is
one we ourselves initially reported.

\subsubsection{A provider-specific failure rate that was a labelling
artifact}
\label{sec:mmlu8b}

On the MMLU cell for the 8B model, Provider~C appeared to show a
13.8\% silent-failure rate against a peer maximum of 0.7\% --- a
larger provider gap than anything else in our data. It is not real.

The mechanism is a labelling asymmetry interacting with our own
taxonomy. On this cell all providers frequently exhaust the 64-token
budget before emitting a parseable letter. Peers report those
generations as \texttt{finish\_reason=length}, which
Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} classifies as a budget cap --- our
artifact, excluded from the reliability axis. Provider~C never emits
\texttt{length} here; it reports \texttt{stop} on all 900 responses.
The identical event therefore enters our taxonomy as a \emph{silent
failure} for Provider~C and an \emph{excluded budget cap} for
everyone else, and the 0.7\% ``peer maximum'' is not a measurement of
peer behaviour but what remains of it after the classifier has routed
it elsewhere.

Scored uniformly across providers --- a cap-hit that fails to emit a
parseable answer --- Provider~C is unremarkable: 13.8\% at ICC(1,1)
0.935, fourth of six by rate and third by item-level determinism, with
Groq (24.6\%, 0.988) and WandB (14.0\%, 0.963) above it on both.
Appendix~\ref{app:mmlu8b} gives all six providers.

This is the clearest demonstration in our data of the paper's own
thesis: a serving-level \emph{reporting} difference, not a
serving-level failure, manufactured a provider gap of more than an
order of magnitude, exactly as our pre-registration warned before the
analysis was finalised. The defensible reading of Provider~C's 8B
behaviour is a \texttt{finish\_reason} conformance bug --- it never
emits \texttt{length} where every peer does --- plus a modest
answer-formatting gap. That is a real defect; it is simply not the one
we first took it for.

\subsubsection{Audit protocol in practice}
\label{sec:audit}

Second, we flagged four candidate silent failures on the 8B model
during a broader provider survey (Section~\ref{sec:breadth}), on two
providers other than Provider~C. On manual inspection of the raw
completion text, all four were complete, correct answers: each ended
with a phrase such as ``The final answer is: \$X'' rather than the
expected delimiter, giving the correct numeric answer in full. None
was actually truncated: the four completions ran to 216, 163, 180 and
259 tokens, all far below the 1024-token budget, and each closed with
a stated numeric answer matching the gold answer (one on Novita, three
on WandB).
We had encountered the identical failure mode once before, during a
smaller pilot run, and had already corrected our audit protocol to
catch it; the fact that it recurred at full scale on a different
provider pair confirms that this is a systematic property of how the
8B model closes its answers, not a one-off annotation error, and that
automated completeness flags on this model require verification
before being reported as findings. We report both the automated flag
rate and the audited, corrected rate throughout this paper, and every
reliability number in Table~\ref{tab:anchor} is the audited figure.

\subsection{Breadth: Is This Provider-Specific?}
\label{sec:breadth}

To test whether the pathology is unique to Provider~C, we swept all 24
canonical deployments live at sweep time --- 6 of the 8 models
surveyed, the other two having no live canonical provider --- at 100
GSM8K items each. Three automated flags were raised; after the
Section~\ref{sec:audit} audit, one survived: Provider~C on
Llama-3.3-70B, leaving it the only confirmed truncator across all 24
(per-model breakdown in Appendix~\ref{app:breadth}). This sweep is
also what re-runs the 20\% GSM8K rate in Table~\ref{tab:anchor} on a
disjoint item sample, though it shares the harness and aggregator of
the primary run and so tests item-set contingency rather than
providing independent replication.

Read the null with its power in mind: at 100 items, a provider
truncating at 2\% is very likely caught, one truncating at 0.5\% less
than half the time. ``No second instance'' bounds pathologies at
Provider~C's scale, not rarer ones.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A Reporting Checklist}
\label{sec:checklist}

Our results argue for treating provider identity as a first-class
variable in any API-based LLM evaluation, alongside model name,
prompt, and decoding parameters. The full checklist is in Appendix~\ref{app:checklist}: six fields ---
model identifier, provider identifier, disclosed quantization,
snapshot date, fallback routing policy, and repeats per item --- that
we recommend every API-based evaluation state explicitly. It is a
narrow addition to existing reporting practice \citep{liang2023helm},
not a replacement for it. The last field carries the weight of
Section~\ref{sec:determinism}: studies relying on temperature-zero,
single-trial evaluation should run a small number of repeats per item
specifically to separate a stochastic serving glitch from a
deterministic, reproducible defect, since only the latter is certain
to recur.

The central reliability finding concerns one provider. We read this
not as a weakness to argue around but as the honest output of a broad,
audited search: the contribution is the measurement protocol and audit
discipline that found it, re-ran it on a disjoint sample, ruled out
the obvious alternative explanation, and searched 23 other deployments
without a second instance. Whether comparable pathologies exist under
other models, benchmarks, or conditions is a question our method is
built to answer, not one we claim to have closed.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Under our pre-registered criterion, no provider pair separates on
accuracy on GSM8K, MMLU, or HumanEval. That single sentence is what a
conventional write-up would report, and it is the least informative
true thing we can say. Analysed with the pairing the design already
provides and with an equivalence test, the three benchmarks come apart:
GSM8K affirmatively supports provider-invariance within $\pm$3
percentage points, MMLU supports neither separation nor equivalence,
and HumanEval yields an uncorrected paired signal that exceeds the
pre-registered magnitude threshold and that we flag for a confirmatory
test rather than claim. The choice of test, not the data, determines
which of those three conclusions a reader sees.

Reliability is less equivocal. One provider shows a real,
content-triggered overt truncation pathology, deterministic at the
item level, not explained by output length, and reproduced on a
disjoint item sample. A breadth search across 24 deployments and six
models found no second instance, which we report as evidence of the
method's reach --- bounded by its power --- rather than as a closed
question. Against that, two apparent reliability findings in our own
data did not survive audit, including one where a
\texttt{finish\_reason} labelling difference manufactured an
order-of-magnitude provider gap that does not exist. Provider
identity, disclosed precision, snapshot date, repeat count, and the
statistical test specified in advance are not incidental details of an
API-based evaluation; our results show that each of them can be the
difference between a real finding and an artifact.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Statistical limitations.} Our pre-registered accuracy test is
weaker than the design deserved, and we report the consequences rather
than the test alone. Requiring two 95\% intervals to be disjoint is
conservative relative to a direct test on the difference; the design
is paired but the pre-registered analysis treats providers as
independent, discarding power; and our intervals are computed over
calls, so the three strongly correlated temperature-0 repeats of an
item are counted as independent observations and the intervals are
correspondingly narrower than an independent-observation reading would
justify. The paired re-analysis we report as a corrective is itself
uncorrected for the 36 implicit pairwise comparisons among nine
providers and selects the extreme pair post hoc, so it is
anti-conservative in the opposite direction; neither analysis alone
settles the HumanEval cell. We report no confidence interval on any
truncation rate in Table~\ref{tab:anchor}, which is a real asymmetry
against the accuracy axis, where intervals are reported throughout.

\textbf{Limitations of the consistency coefficient.} ICC(1,1) assumes
a continuous homoscedastic outcome and we apply it to a binary one
(Appendix~\ref{app:icc}). More importantly, its discriminant validity
is untested: our only comparison condition is peer providers with
\emph{zero} truncations, for which the coefficient is undefined by
construction rather than informatively low. We have no condition with
a nonzero but item-independent failure rate, so we have not
demonstrated that the coefficient would return a low value where it
should. The MMLU/8B analysis in Section~\ref{sec:mmlu8b} is a
cautionary instance: on that cell peers do have nonzero rates, and
their coefficients are as high as Provider~C's, which is why we
withdrew the cell rather than report it as discriminating.

\textbf{Detector sensitivity.} Our breadth sweep found no second
truncating provider, but we have not characterised our detector's
false-negative rate, and we did not validate it against injected
failures. Combined with 100 items per deployment, the null bounds the
prevalence of pathologies at Provider~C's magnitude and says little
about rarer ones.

\textbf{Scope.} Our central reliability claim rests on one provider;
the breadth search tests generality but the finding remains anchored
to a single instance rather than a class. We evaluate two models,
selected because they were the only candidates among eight surveyed
with sufficient live provider coverage; this is itself a finding about
the marketplace, but it limits generalization. All data was collected
through a single aggregator (OpenRouter) and may not transfer to other
routing marketplaces. Our data is snapshot-dated: provider
availability, disclosed quantization, and serving behavior all changed
during collection, including one provider that became entirely
unavailable and one that experienced a transient multi-hour outage,
both handled by exclusion and documentation rather than folded into
the reliability finding.

\textbf{Mechanism and cost.} We establish that Provider~C's truncation
is deterministic and item-specific, but without access to the serving
infrastructure we cannot identify its root cause, whether a batching
policy, a timeout configuration, or something else. Finally, our audit
protocol is manual and does not scale to arbitrarily large studies; we
view this as a genuine cost of the rigor we argue for, not an
incidental limitation --- though Section~\ref{sec:mmlu8b} is evidence
that skipping it is more expensive.

\section{Item-Level Determinism as an Intraclass Correlation}
\label{app:icc}

This appendix gives the full form of the coefficient summarized in
Section~\ref{sec:determinism}.

The binomial comparison in Section~\ref{sec:determinism} is specific
to GSM8K and to a single repeat count. To generalize it across
benchmarks we apply a standard measurement-theory statistic rather
than introducing a new one: the one-way random-effects intraclass
correlation coefficient
\[
\mathrm{ICC}(1,1) = \frac{\mathrm{MS}_B - \mathrm{MS}_W}
{\mathrm{MS}_B + (k-1)\,\mathrm{MS}_W},
\]
\noindent \citep{shrout1979icc, mcgraw1996icc} where each item is a
subject, each of its $k{=}3$ trials a replicate measurement of a
binary outcome (truncated or not), and $\mathrm{MS}_B$,
$\mathrm{MS}_W$ are the between- and within-item mean squares from a
one-way ANOVA on the truncation indicator. It gives the fraction of
outcome variance attributable to item identity rather than to
repeat-to-repeat noise within an item: near~0, failures are
effectively random with respect to which item is processed; near~1,
they are almost entirely determined by it. We refer to ICC(1,1) on a
truncation indicator as a Failure Consistency Coefficient (FCC); the
name denotes an application, not a new statistic. We report 95\%
confidence intervals via item-level bootstrap (10,000 resamples).

Two caveats belong with the estimate. ICC(1,1) is derived under a
continuous, homoscedastic random-effects model, whereas our outcome is
binary, so within-item variance is mechanically tied to the item mean;
a latent-scale ICC from a random-effects logistic model, or a
kappa-family statistic, would rest on assumptions better matched to
the data, and we would expect any cross-cell \emph{ordering} of these
values to be sensitive to that choice. And $k{=}3$ is a thin basis for
an ICC: our bootstrap resamples items only, so it captures
item-sampling uncertainty but propagates replicate-level uncertainty
poorly. We therefore use the coefficient to support a qualitative
claim --- failures are substantially an item property --- and not to
rank cells against one another.

\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Cell} & \textbf{Provider~C FCC} & \textbf{Peer FCC} \\
\midrule
GSM8K / 70B      & 0.698 [0.604, 0.783] & undefined \\
HumanEval / 70B  & 0.751 [0.550, 0.887] & undefined \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{ICC(1,1) with 95\% bootstrap CI for Provider~C's
overt truncation outcome, on the two cells where it truncates. Peers
(Nebius on GSM8K, AkashML on HumanEval) record zero overt truncations,
so the outcome has no variance to partition and the coefficient is
undefined ($0/0$) --- a degenerate case, not a discriminant result: it
conveys only what Table~\ref{tab:anchor}'s peer-max column already
reports, and leaves the coefficient's ability to separate
deterministic from stochastic failure untested
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).}
\label{tab:fcc}
\end{center}

Both intervals in Table~\ref{tab:fcc} exclude 0, confirming with a
standard reliability statistic what Section~\ref{sec:determinism}
showed for GSM8K alone: truncation is substantially an item property,
not a repeat-to-repeat coin flip. GSM8K's 0.698 matches that section's
observation that some items truncate on only one or two of three
trials rather than all-or-nothing --- a real but noisier item effect
over some residual stochasticity. HumanEval's estimate rests on 39
truncation events across 164 items, so we quote its correspondingly
wide interval ([0.550, 0.887]) rather than the point estimate.

deterministic cell. That entry is withdrawn:
Section~\ref{sec:finish_reason} shows the underlying event is not a
Provider~C failure, and, decisively for the coefficient's
interpretation, peers on that cell return values at or above
Provider~C's on the identical event --- so 0.935 measured item
difficulty under a fixed token budget, a property every provider in
the cell shares.
